The **Defence Force of Varahmehr** (Mehrāni: نیروی دفاعی ورهمهر, *Niru-ye Defāʿī-ye Varahmehr*; abbr. **DFV**) serves as the combined military of the [[varahmehr|Federal Republic of Varahmehr]], established upon the signing of the Federal Compact of Varahmehr on 3 March 1878. It is organised into four principal branches: the National Force (*Sepāh-e Mellī*), the six Provincial Forces (*Niru-ye Ostānī*), the Naval Force (*Niru-ye Daryāyi*), and the National Air Service (*Hava-ye Mellī*), the last of which was upgraded from a research division to an operational development programme by the Majles-e Mellī in December 1929.

The DFV operates under the constitutional authority of the Vazir-e Azam as a commander-in-chief and is subject to the legislative oversight of the Majles-e Mellī. Its stated doctrine - *Hefāzat-e Peymān*, Defence of the Compact - holds that the DFV exists to make the cost of any incursion into Varahmehr’s territory prohibitive rather than to project power beyond its borders. This posture reflects the founding circumstances of the federation and is reinforced constitutionally by the prohibition on wars of aggression embedded in the Peymān-e Mellī.

## History

The military traditions that formed the DFV predate the federation by centuries. During the Six Lands period, the pre-federal territories maintained separate armed forces of varying character and capability, engaging in a pattern of limited conflict and enforced coexistence that produced the institutional memory from which the DFV was ultimately built. Ahanestan’s iron clans maintained the region’s most formidable land forces, organized around clan structures and supplemented by Mehrāni settler militias in the valley districts. The clan armies conducted two documented raids against Nourestan’s plateau settlements in the century preceding the Compact, both of which were resolved through commercial agreements rather than decisive military engagement - establishing an early precedent that economic instruments were more durable than military ones in settling inter-territorial disputes. Nourestan maintained a smaller but better-organized force, drawing on its legal and administrative tradition to produce a structured officer class and a disciplined infantry that was outmatched in the desert terrain by Ahanestan’s clan warriors but considerably more effective in set-piece engagements on the plateau. Dashtestan’s *Sowārs-e Dasht* - the plains riders - were the region’s premier cavalry force, capable of rapid deployment across the agricultural plain and deeply embedded in the Dashti clan structure, but unsuited to the highland terrain that defined most serious military confrontations. Shushestan, militarily the weakest of the six territories, maintained commercial relationships and legal frameworks as its primary security instruments, supplemented by a small coastal guard operating from Shushabad’s port. The signing of the Federal Compact on 3 March 1878 established the constitutional framework for the DFV. The founding negotiators’ primary concern was not external defense but internal restraint - a federal military capable of being directed against any single ostān was precisely the instrument of centralized coercion their republic was designed to prevent. The resolution was the two-layer structure that remains in place today: a federally commanded National Force of limited size, and six constitutionally restricted Provincial Forces that could not be deployed outside their home territories without Assembly approval. This arrangement was the condition of Ahanestan’s accession to the Compact, whose Kuhi clans would not accept federal military authority over their lands without the guarantee that federal forces could not be concentrated against them.

The DFV’s founding years were occupied with administrative challenges of building federal institutions - establishing the Dāneshgāh-e Nezāmi officer academy in Nourabad, standardizing equipment and training across six previously incompatible provincial forces, and negotiating the jurisdictional boundaries between federal and provincial military authority that the Compact had established in principle but not in operational detail. The Constitutional Court’s landmark ruling in the Ahanestan tariff case of the federation’s third decade - in which an Ostāndār’s attempt to levy tariffs on goods from Shushestan was struck down as a federal power - established a precedent for federal supremacy in interstate matters that applied equally to military jurisdiction, clarifying several ambiguities the founding document had left unresolved. The 51-year isolationist period that followed the federation’s administrative consolidation severely limited the DFV’s operational development. The absence of external military engagement preserved institutional structure while denying operational experience, a combination that produced a force well-organized on paper and significantly undertested in practice.

As of December 1929, the DFV is engaged in the most consequential modernization review in its history. The end of isolationism and the discovery of large oil fields requiring infrastructure protection have simultaneously transformed the organization’s strategic environment and exposed the gap between its current capabilities and its emerging responsibilities.

## Structure

Dariush Nāmdār, the Commander General (*Farmāndeh-ye Koll*), serves as the DFV’s senior military officer, commanding the organisation under the constitutional authority of the Vazir-e Azam. Military authority runs from the Commander General through the **Great Staff** (*Setād-e Bozorg*), which advises him and is composed of the three service chiefs - the Chief of the National Force, the Chief of the Naval Force, and the Director of the National Air Service - who collectively hold the highest military rank in their respective branches. The Great Staff functions as the DFV’s planning, operations, and policy headquarters, with each service branch retaining administrative authority and responsibility for force development within its designated sphere. The six Provincial Force commanders hold dual reporting relationships to both the Great Staff and their respective Ostāndārs, a structural ambiguity that reflects the federal-provincial tension embedded in the Compact itself and that has historically been managed through personal relationships rather than resolved through formal clarification. The DFV does not operate regional Theatre Commands of the type common in larger military organisations. Given the federation’s geographic compactness and the constitutional restriction on Provincial Forces operating outside their home territories, operation coordination in peacetime is handled through the Great Staff’s operations bureau. In wartime, the **War Council** (*Shurā-ye Jang*) activates - a body in which each ostān’s senior military commander sits alongside the national commander to coordinate combined operations. Activation requires a formal declaration by the Majles-e Mellī rather than a Chancellor’s order, a constitutional provision that the Great Staff regards as an operational constraint and the Assembly regards as a fundamental safeguard against executive military overreach.

### National Force - Sepāh-e Mellī

The **National Force** (*Sepāh-e Mellī*) is the DFV’s federal standing army, comprising approximately 45,000 professional soldiers and headquartered in Nourabad under the command of Sarlashkar Hormoz Vaziri. It is the only element of the DFV that can be deployed anywhere within Varahmehr’s territory without Assembly authorisation, a provision that makes it the federation’s primary rapid response force for both external threats and internal security emergencies. The National Force is organised into four **divisions** (*Lashkar*) of roughly 10,000 soldiers each, with the remaining manpower distributed across specialist, signal, logistics, engineering, and headquarters support functions. Each Lashkar is a combined-arms formation capable of independent sustained operations, with infantry, artillery, and cavalry operating under a single divisional commander rather than being parcelled by arm of service - a lesson drawn explicitly from the coordination failures of the Great War’s Western Front, absorbed through Farzānestan’s military academic literature during the isolationist period despite the absence of direct operational experience. The National Force’s officer corps is trained at the **Dāneshgāh-e Nezāmi** in Nourabad, the federation’s premier military academy and the institution that standardizes officer education across all DFV branches. The academy draws students from every ostān - including, increasingly, from the Kuhi communities of Ahanestan, whose admission in meaningful numbers represents one of the most visible institutional changes of the past decade and one that the Mardom-e Kār has publicly supported as a corrective to the historically Mehrāni composition of the officer class. Graduates of the Dāneshgāh-e Nezāmi serve a mandatory posting to the National Force before transferring to Provincial Force commands, a requirement that creates cross-ostān professional relationships the Great Staff considers essential to the DFV’s coherence as a federal institution.

### Provincial Forces - Niru-ye Ostānī

The six **Provincial Forces** collectively number approximately 105,000 personnel, making them considerably larger than the National Force. Each is funded jointly by federal and ostān budgets, trained to federal standards established by the Dāneshgāh-e Nezāmi, and commanded by the Ostāndār’s military council under the operational oversight of the Great Staff. The constitutional prohibition on deployment outside home territories without Assembly approval is the defining constraint on the Provincial Forces and the provision that most clearly reflects the founding compact between the federal government and the six ostān-hā.

The **Iron Corps** (*Sepāh-eĀhan*) of Ahanestan, with approximately 30,000 personnel and a budget of 15.1 billion, is the largest and most heavily equipped Provincial Force in the federation. It operates four artillery regiments manufacturing their own field guns in Kuhestan’s foundries, armored car units built in Āhanshahr’s engineering works, and the federation’s most capable mountain infantry - drawn disproportionately from the Kuhi population whose generations of highland life have produced soldiers with an intimate knowledge of the terrain that no training program can replicate. The Iron Corps’ budget reflects both Ahanestan’s industrial capacity and the strategic importance of defending the oil fields whose discovery has transformed the ostān’s economic significance.

The **Nour Guards** (*Gardān-e Nour*) of Nourestan, with approximately 20,000 personnel, functions less as a conventional combat formation than as the DFV’s administrative and signals infrastructure. Nourestan hosts the Dāneshgāh-e Nezāmi and the federal signals network that connects Nourabad to every ostān capital. The Nour Guards operate the only radio-equipped signals battalion in the federation and provide the communications backbone on which all other DFV elements depend during operations. Their combat element - a single well-equipped infantry regiment - is trained to a high standard and deployed primarily in the defense of the capital and federal government facilities. The **Naval Force** of Shushestan, with approximately 20,000 personnel, is discussed separately below due to its distinct operational character. The **Plains Riders** (*Sowārs-e Dasht*) of Dashtestan field approximately 18,000 personnel organized around four cavalry regiments of roughly 800 horses each - the last major operational cavalry force in Varahmehr and a subject of ongoing doctrinal debate between commanders who believe horse cavalry retains operational relevance on the open plains and staff officers who have read the Evrian theatre reports and are privately less certain. The Dashtestan force also maintains the **Village Guard** (*Dehyāri-ye Dashtī*), a militia system organized around clan structures that predate the Compact and represents the most genuinely decentralized military organization in the federation. The **Scholars’ Corps** (*Gardān-e Dānesh*) of Farzānestan contributes 10,000 personnel primarily through its military engineering unit, cartographic and survey corps, military medical service, and the signals research unit at Golestan’s academy. The **Free Company** (*Gardān-e Āzād*) of Azadestan-e Kuchak, with 7,000 personnel deliberately kept minimal by Āzādi-khāhān doctrine, specialises in irregular warfare, coastal interdiction, and what its own doctrine calls *jang-e sayeh* - shadow warfare. It maintains the only dedicated signals intelligence unit in the DFV, operating out of Rāhgozar under the Ostāndār’s authority in a relationship with the federal counterintelligence structure that is close enough to be useful and loose enough to maintain deniability.

### Naval Force - Niru-ye Daryāyi

The **Naval Force** (*Niru-ye Daryāyi*) is concentrated in Shushestan and commanded by Daryābod Kamāl Shirāzi, operating primarily from Shushabad and Bandar Mehr. It is, by the frank assessment of the Farmāndeh-ye Koll’s annual report, the most significantly underdeveloped element of the DFV relative to Varahmehr’s strategic requirements. The Naval Force’s primary assets consist of coastal patrol vessels and merchant escort craft. Its operational range is limited to coastal and near-coastal waters, with sustained blue-water operations beyond the Daria Bay beyond its current capability. The Naval Force’s officer corps is widely regarded as the DFV’s most cosmopolitan, drawing on Shushestan’s centuries of maritime commercial tradition and the multilingual professional culture of Shushabad’s port. Naval officers are the most likely members of the DFV to have had contact with foreign military counterparts during the isolationist period through commercial maritime channels, and their institutional knowledge of regional naval capabilities is considered more current than that of the land forces.

### National Air Service - Hava-ye Mellī

The **National Air Service** (*Hava-ye Mellī*) was established during the isolationist period as a research and development division rather than an operational air arm, a designation that reflected the Assembly’s historical reluctance to fund military capabilities whose strategic value remained theoretical in the absence of any external engagement. As of December 1929 it possesses no operational aircraft.

On 13 December 1929, the Majles-e Mellī voted 33 to 24 to upgrade the National Air Service from research division to operational development programme, with an 18-month mandate to achieve initial operational capability by mid-1931. Behruz Sepehr, the division’s research director and a graduate of Farzānestan’s Golestan academy, was appointed Director of Aviation Development with emergency budget authority and a recruitment mandate that reached immediately into the engineering faculties of Farzānestan’s academies and the precision manufacturing workshops of Ahanestan’s industrial sector. The programme’s doctrine - contested between those who believe aircraft should be distributed as infantry support assets and those who argue for independent concentration - remains unsolved, a debate that Sepehr has noted in his writing will become urgent approximately twelve months before the aircraft themselves are ready to fly.

## Doctrine and Strategy

The DFV’s foundational doctrine is designated **Hefāzat-e Peymān** - Defence of the Compact - a formulation that reflects both the military’s constitutional mandate and the specific historical circumstances of its creation. The doctrine holds that Varahmehr will not initiate offensive military operations, that its military exists to impose costs on potential aggressors rather than to project power, and that the federation’s geographic diversity demands a layered defensive posture organised around terrain knowledge rather than concentrated firepower. This posture is reinforced by the constitutional prohibition on wars of aggression in the Peymān-e Mellī, which the Dīvan-e Adl has consistently interpreted as binding on the executive regardless of the strategic justification offered. The doctrine’s three layers correspond directly to the DFV’s structural organization. The Provincial Forces provide territorial depth - forces that know their home terrain with a granularity that external aggressors cannot replicate, capable of sustained local resistance that imposes costs disproportionate to the territory contested. The National Force provided mobile response capability, able to concentrate strength where the threat materializes without being committed to fixed defensive positions. The War Council provides the coordination mechanism that integrates these layers under unified command when the threat is existential rather than manageable by individual ostān forces.

The doctrine’s central vulnerability - recognized in the Great Staff’s assessments and widely discussed in Farzānestan’s military academic community - is its assumption that threats will arrive in a form the existing structure can address. The 1924 oil discovery created infrastructure that required protection beyond the established defensive perimeter. And the end of isolationism has opened diplomatic relationships that carry security implications the doctrine was not designed to address. The DFV’s current modernization program is, in effect, an attempt to update a doctrine written for an isolated agrarian federation to serve a republic that is rapidly becoming neither.

## Manning and Conscription

The DFV operates on a mixed professional-conscript model that reflects a constitutional compromise between the federalist instinct toward standardized national service and the provincial instinct toward local control of military manpower. The National Force is entirely professional - volunteer soldiers serving under federal contract with standardized pay scales, training requirements, and conditions of service governed by federal statute. The Provincial Forces combine a permanent professional cadre with reserve conscripts drawn from each ostān’s service-eligible population under a rotating obligation system that varies in duration and intensity by ostān within federal minimum standards.

Conscription has been a recurring subject of Assembly debate throughout the federation’s history. The Āzādi-khāhān have consistently opposed mandatory service as a violation of individual sovereignty over one’s own person and time - a position they have maintained with characteristic consistency even during debates where the security arguments for conscription have been at their strongest. The Risheh-dārān and Mardom-e Kār have consistently defended it as an expression of civic obligation and as the mechanism that makes the Provincial Forces a genuine expression of the communities they defend. The compromise embedded in current practice - mandatory reserve service with significant local variation in its application - has produced a system that fully satisfies neither side, and both have learned to tolerate it as the least bad available outcome.

The service-eligible population - citizens between approximately 18 and 35 - numbers roughly 14.2 million, representing approximately 27% of the total population. Approximately 150,000 active military personnel represent a participation rate of around 1.06% of the eligible population. Reserve personnel of approximately 380,000 bring the total trained military population to around 530,000, or around 3.7% of the service-eligible cohort.
